Avoid Flipkart

From delayed Flipkart deliveries to unanswered questions

I placed five orders and bought a membership. What followed was an unhurried masterclass in bureaucratic theatre, algorithmic gaslighting, and courier metaphysics.

Four distinct acts:

The transaction

I parted with legal tender. Flipkart provided estimated delivery dates that functioned, much like astrology, as poetic mood boards rather than temporal commitments.

The horoscope

Promised dates vanished into thin air. Tracking calmly insisted I had personally petitioned Flipkart to detain my own parcels in a warehouse.

The VIP darshan

Paid “no queue, no bot” priority support deposited me behind a queue of sixty-four people to debate fruit orthography with an automated script.

The resolution

A delivery complaint stamped “resolved” on 6 November for a missing stand still expected on 11 November, while a case for a mouse delivered weeks ago remained open.

On Flipkart BLACK priority support

Promised 24x7 priority support with “no queue, no bot.” In reality: an impenetrable VIP barricade where an algorithm assures you it is a living human, refuses to count letters in words, and repeatedly advises you to inspect WhatsApp by 6 p.m. Six weeks and multiple executive escalations later, nobody has clarified when the benefit actually begins.

One customer.
Many tomorrows.

An independent account · September–November 2026

12 min read

In standard economic theory, e-commerce is a remarkably simple transaction: you enter your CVV, complete the mandatory OTP dance, and eventually a gentleman on a Hero Splendor calls asking for a landmark near a dairy booth to hand you a cardboard box.

Flipkart does not subscribe to standard economic theory. In Flipkart’s operational cosmology, ordering a computer mouse is not a retail transaction; it is an epic, six-week spiritual expedition into the relativity of space, time, and Indian postal metaphysics.

Cosmologists tell us that space is big—vastly, hugely, mind-bogglingly big. Flipkart’s supply chain has managed to recreate this infinite void entirely within municipal Pune. Across six weeks, five orders produced an unbroken cascade of chronological fiction: delivery dates that shifted like desert dunes, three near-identical notices declaring a parcel simultaneously lost, damaged, and undergoing “systemic repromise,” an impromptu fruit-spelling examination of a corporate chatbot, a delivery complaint stamped “resolved” while the merchandise remained stubbornly absent, and an escalation to the chief executives of Flipkart and Walmart that moved with all the thundering urgency of a continental shelf. The automated notification emails arrived with ruthless punctuality; only the physical cardboard struggled with the Newtonian laws of motion.

The airport scene and product illustrations on this page are visual aids, not actual photographs of parcels wandering through Maharashtra.

The scenic route.

Five orders as recorded in Flipkart’s dispatch notices and account pages.

The mouse

Logitech MX Vertical

Promised for 15 October, delivered on 21 October.

Six days late for an in-stock plastic ergonomic mouse. But the real artistic masterpiece was the internal tracking status: “Delivery rescheduled as per customer request.”

Consider the staggering existential confidence required to manufacture this claim. What sort of individual wakes up on a Tuesday morning, inspects their aching wrist, and thinks: “You know what? My carpal tunnel is feeling far too comfortable today. Let me contact Flipkart and formally petition them to detain my ergonomic mouse at a distribution depot for six more calendar days”? Did I sleepwalk to the warehouse? Did my subconscious file an astral affidavit? If I possessed the psychic authority to reschedule physical shipments into the future, I would have used it to ensure the parcel arrived last week.

To assist with the delay, customer support helpfully advised: “if delivery executive will call you the number may reflect as a spam and last three digits will be 777.” Flipkart’s logistics architecture is so singularly advanced that its couriers arrive cloaked in the caller ID of a telephone scammer. Even after the mouse finally crossed the threshold on 21 October, an internal support issue remained proudly open on my account with a promised resolution deadline of: “Tomorrow, Nov 07”—seventeen days after delivery had already occurred.

The licences

Microsoft 365 Personal × 5

Ordered 25 September, delivery reported on 25 October.

Thirty full days. Seven hundred and twenty hours. To transmit an electronic software product key across the internet.

In thirty days, an Indian student can complete an entire coaching module, solve a decade of previous year physics papers, and undergo two distinct identity crises. In thirty days, a caterpillar pupates, emerges, and dies of old age. In thirty days, one could walk barefoot from Bengaluru to New Delhi, chisel five 25-character Microsoft activation codes onto solid granite tablets, and carry them home on a bullock cart. What was the digital key doing for an entire month? Was it stuck in traffic at Silk Board junction? Did the string of alphanumeric characters stop at a roadside dhaba for butter naan and lassi?

The support chat greeted my inquiries with tranquil zen: “Due to logistics issues, it couldn't be delivered today.” When I asked how a digital license code suffers a fifteen-day logistical issue, and whether the company was shutting down or going bankrupt, support replied: “I reassure you that your order is safe and we are doing our absolute best.” The order page eventually listed five delivered entries and four cancelled entries for those five licences—nine discrete outcomes for five items. The replacement request was marked complete, which tidily settles the paperwork without providing any clues as to where the digital bits spent October.

The extender

TP-Link Wi-Fi range extender

Due 22 October, delivered on 29 October.

Due on 22 October. At 1:19 a.m. on 23 October—exactly seventy-nine minutes after the day had officially died—an automated email arrived to inform me that yesterday’s delivery was running late.

This is not predictive logistics. This is an unprompted 2 a.m. text from a former partner saying: “Hey... so about yesterday... it didn’t work out.” Informing a customer in the dead of night that yesterday failed to happen is not supply-chain management; it is historical revisionism. The delivery calendar was emailing its condolences from beyond the grave.

The extender finally arrived on 29 October, seven days late. Yet at 1:14 a.m. the following morning, a chat agent named Gaurav cheerfully announced: “I've checked your TP-Link RE200 WiFi Router, and it is scheduled for delivery tomorrow, 30 Oct, Friday.” Having successfully delivered the item yesterday, Flipkart was now earnestly making arrangements to deliver it tomorrow. Time at Flipkart does not travel in a linear arrow; it pools, evaporates, and occasionally flows backward into the weekend.

The stand

Acer ZC.A01SI.179 laptop stand

Promised for 21 October, moved to 22 October, and then systematically shuffled forward into infinity.

When pressed on why the date kept advancing, customer support unveiled the definitive corporate euphemism: “systemic repromise being done.” It is a phrase worthy of the Sirius Cybernetics Corporation. In plain English, it means: The computer noticed that reality did not conform to its previous fantasy, so it has generated a fresh fantasy dated twenty-four hours later.

For two straight weeks, support agents recited like a liturgical chant: “Please don't worry, your order is safe and there is no indication of cancellation.” Then, on 4 November, thirty-two minutes apart, Flipkart delivered two mutually destructive explanations. At 9:41 p.m., an email announced the parcel “may have been lost or damaged while on its way to you” and promised a replacement. At 10:13 p.m., a second notice declared the stand “could not be successfully delivered as it did not meet the product delivery guidelines.”

A folded piece of stamped aluminium had failed the moral and behavioural guidelines for being delivered. Did it fail the dress code? Did it display insufficient humility at the Prabhat Road hub? The original order was summarily cancelled, and a replacement appeared, expected on 11 November. On 6 November, the delivery complaint was stamped “resolved.” The complaint was officially dead and buried while the physical stand remained entirely theoretical.

The trimmer

Philips MG3760/33 multigroomer

Ordered 28 October, expected 4 November. On 6 November, the order page maintained with stoic conviction that it was still “out for delivery.”

A parcel described as “out for delivery” forty-eight hours past its deadline is not out for delivery. It has moved out, changed its name, and is pursuing other spiritual interests. In the support chat, an agent named Pooja promised that a priority ticket had been raised and that a definitive update would arrive on WhatsApp by 6 p.m. When I asked the reasonable question: “Till end of time? Then again next day”, Pooja repeated the assurance. Moments later, the bot typed: “Please ignore above line.”

It is the first recorded instance of an e-commerce corporation attempting to un-say its own promises in real time. The captured records contain no delivery confirmation. The trimmer is presumably somewhere out in the cosmos, shaving the void.

The email trail.

Excerpts from private correspondence. Commentary is mine; absurdity was Flipkart’s.

14 October 2026

Three emails, one quantum parcel

Flipkart wrote to inform me that my Microsoft shipment “may have been lost or damaged while on its way to you” and a replacement would be dispatched. By 17 October, I had received three near-identical notices, while no replacement had actually left the depot.

“Lost or damaged” is a magnificent phrase. It spans the entire philosophical spectrum of non-existence. The package might have fallen into an open Bangalore storm drain; it might have been consumed by a sacred cow; it might have dissolved into pure radiant energy. What it was categorically not doing was arriving in my inbox. Three near-identical notices that your parcel is theoretically destroyed is wonderful if you collect automated corporate apologies, but unhelpful when you actually need to open a spreadsheet.

17 October 2026

The customer who never was

Internal tracking marked my orders rescheduled “at customer request.” I had spent a week sending frantic messages pleading with anyone with a pulse to put the box on a two-wheeler.

To then receive an automated update claiming that I personally petitioned the company to delay my own order is gaslighting of galactic proportions. It is like being slapped across the face by a stranger who then turns to the crowd and announces: “He asked me to do that, he’s very fond of cheek stimulation.” If my pending orders have developed the legal autonomy to negotiate their own delivery dates, Flipkart should immediately put them on the board of directors.

That day I sent a formal grievance headed “3 orders undelivered, wrong dates blamed on me - please fix” to grievance.officer@flipkart.com, saluting “Mr Karthik R.” I asked for the products, the correction of the records, and one human whose phone works. It was a modest list. No acknowledgement appears in the captured mail.

20 October 2026

Escalating to the stratosphere

Having exhausted the chatbots, I escalated directly to Kalyan Krishnamurthy, Group CEO of Flipkart, and John Furner, President and CEO of Walmart Inc. I copied the Flipkart grievance mailbox and the Walmart directors' address, attached order numbers and dates, and asked for a single accountable human.

An email thread with that distribution list could ratify an international extradition treaty. The job titles had reached the stratosphere. The reply, that same day, came from Customer Support, concerned the Logitech mouse, and requested time until 21 October, 4:23 p.m. It did not mention the email, the grievance, or either CEO, which is one way to keep a conversation focused.

20 October 2026

The strawberry Turing test

“There are 3 r's in strawberry! I'm a real agent here to help you.”

Having purchased a 12-month Flipkart BLACK membership promising “Priority Customer Support 24x7 no queue no bot,” I opened the priority channel. I was immediately deposited into a waiting line behind dozens of other priority members.

To establish whether I was conversing with a sentient human being or an uninspired algorithm running on a potato, I laid a deliberate trap: “If you are human please prove by telling number of r's in strawberrry” (deliberately spelling it with three r’s at the end, making four in total).

Agent Zaid instantly typed: “There are 3 r's in strawberry! I'm a real agent here to help you. I've checked your Acer laptop stand, and it's being prioritized for delivery by 26 Oct 2026.”

The script had normalized the word to the dictionary, answered the standard internet riddle, asserted its humanity, and copy-pasted the exact canned delivery template it had used two minutes earlier. When I pointed out: “Umm I said strawberrry so you are a bot”, Zaid responded without blinking: “I hear you, and I'm sorry for the frustration. I am a real agent here to help you personally. I've checked your Acer laptop stand, and it's being prioritized for delivery by 26 Oct 2026.” If this is “no queue, no bot,” I would be terrified to see what Flipkart considers an actual bot. It probably demands biometric verification before failing an elementary spelling test.

23 October 2026

The specialist who solved yesterday

I finally reached a living, breathing human representative on the phone. They were exceedingly polite, confirmed they could see my Logitech mouse order, and then gently explained that my other pending orders belonged to other teams and could not be discussed on that call.

The mouse had already been delivered forty-eight hours earlier. I had successfully pierced the corporate veil only to speak with an agent whose jurisdiction was strictly confined to the only problem that no longer existed. If Flipkart operated an emergency ambulance service, the paramedics would arrive exclusively at the homes of people who had already made a full recovery and were currently eating parathas.

After the call I wrote to both CEOs again, because the agent had told me to expect contact from other teams. No written reply followed. The mouse, at least, had been delivered. Its department had done its job, and its jurisdiction was now closed.

26 October 2026

The lunch-break denial

“We see you havent contacted our support team about your concern.”

An automated email arrived declaring my case was now “top priority” and a specialist would call within 24 hours. Naturally, that call never materialized.

Instead, a formal declaration arrived from the Grievance Officer: “We see you havent contacted our support team about your concern.” This was printed directly at the bottom of an email thread containing fourteen prior support messages from Flipkart agents.

This is the absolute pinnacle of Indian bureaucratic theatre. It is the spiritual equivalent of walking up to an inquiry counter, presenting your passbook, and being told: “Aapka account hi nahi hai, lunch ke baad aana.” Entering the grievance department is like stepping through a wormhole: all preceding history is wiped clean. The previous fourteen customer service representatives who wrote to me had apparently done so purely in a personal capacity as pen pals. To prove I had contacted support, I would need to forward the support thread to the grievance desk, who would almost certainly reply to inform me that email had not yet been invented.

28–29 October 2026

The notice, and the delay it suffered

On 28 October I wrote to both CEOs and the grievance mailbox under the subject “Two paid orders undelivered, grievance of 17 October unanswered, three escalations with no reply, legal notice.” The subject line was doing a lot of work, but it was accurate.

The email asked for a named owner, dispatch dates, the order-event audit trail, answers on the BLACK membership, and a reply by 5 p.m. on 30 October. It cited Rule 4(5) of the Consumer (E-Commerce) Rules 2020 requiring a 48-hour grievance acknowledgement. It also put the leadership on notice that I intended to institute proceedings before the Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission. That is not a legal finding; it is a person writing a very long email and pressing send.

The next day, Flipkart reported delivering the Wi-Fi extender. The extender had been due on 22 October. Whatever moved it — the notice, the planetary alignment, or a courier finally locating the correct street — the parcel shifted the day after the words “Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission” entered the thread.

When I tried to send a follow-up to the grievance address, Gmail bounced back: “Temporary System Problem... Delivery delayed... will continue trying for 46 hours.” The cosmic loop was complete. My complaint about delivery delays had suffered a delivery delay. Somewhere, a mail server had adopted the local management style.

2 November 2026

The reply was mine

“I give up, it seems no one at Flipkart or Walmart gives a damn about FK Customers. Thanks for the cautionary tale. Bess your heart.”

Sixteen days after the grievance, thirteen after the CEO escalation, and five after the notice, the thread contained no message from a named recipient. I closed it myself.

The typo is mine. I stand by it. A company that will not answer deserves correspondence of equivalent care.

1–4 November 2026

Three deadlines, one concerned team

Three incident emails arrived. All three opened identically: “We have escalated your issue to the concerned team.” I have begun to picture the concerned team as a small, extremely busy office with no phone, no headcount, and no output, staffed entirely by the word “concerned.”

Each promised to resolve the problem by 7 November: at 11:55 a.m., at 6:37 p.m., and at 9:00 p.m. Three deadlines for one day is not a schedule. It is a raffle with apologies.

The Logitech support issue remained open throughout. Its card read: “Open | Resolution by Tomorrow, Nov 07”. The app has invented a new tense in which tomorrow is a fixed calendar date. The mouse had been delivered two weeks earlier and was, by then, the only participant in this story with nothing left to resolve.

4 November 2026

Two diagnoses, thirty-two minutes

At 9:41 p.m. on 4 November, Flipkart wrote that the Acer stand “may have been lost or damaged while on its way to you” and that a new item would be shipped. At 10:13 p.m., it wrote that the item “could not be successfully delivered as it did not meet the product delivery guidelines”, and that the seller would work on a replacement.

Two diagnoses, one missing parcel. “Lost or damaged” is a flexible condition; “did not meet the product delivery guidelines” is a bureaucratic way of saying the parcel was rejected by the concept of delivery. A laptop stand had failed the entrance exam for arriving.

The original order was cancelled and a replacement was scheduled for 11 November. On 6 November the delivery complaint was marked resolved. The complaint took less time to resolve than the stand has taken to exist.

6 November 2026

The word “resolved”

On 6 November the account’s issue list showed the Acer delivery complaint as “resolved”. The Acer replacement was still expected on 11 November. The stand had never arrived, yet the complaint was officially closed, which suggests that in this vocabulary the word describes the administrative comfort of a file rather than the physical reality of a parcel.

At the same review, the Logitech support issue was still stubbornly marked “open”, with a promised deadline of 7 November. The mouse had arrived on 21 October, more than two weeks earlier. In Flipkart’s filing cabinet, a delivered product requires ongoing investigation, while an undelivered one is successfully settled. The grievance of 17 October had no acknowledgement in the captured mail.

Somewhere in this filing system there is a definition of “resolved” that would keep a university philosophy department occupied for a decade. I have not been shown it. I have been shown the label.

6 November 2026

The leadership file

Written to: the grievance mailbox, 17 October. The Group CEO of Flipkart and the President and CEO of Walmart, 20 October. Both of them again, plus the grievance officer and the Walmart directors’ address, 28 October. A closing reply to the same thread, 2 November.

Received in reply, as of 6 November: automated incident updates from Customer Support, one phone call about a mouse that had already arrived, and no message from any named recipient. No acknowledgement or closure notice for the grievance appears in the captured mail through 6 November.

There is a difference between an email being received and a company being reached. Flipkart has mastered the first.

The verdict.

What did we learn? Absolutely nothing we wanted to.

People often ask me if there’s a moral to this story. There is: if you ever feel the sudden urge to buy something on Flipkart, take your credit card out into the garden, set fire to it, and tell yourself the transaction was rescheduled at your own request.

You still won’t get your parcel, but at least your ceiling fan won’t have to listen to you arguing with an automated script named Zaid about how many letters are in a strawberry.

Let’s review the final mathematics of Indian quantum retail:

  • Five orders placed. Three arrived late, one was cancelled after failing the entrance exam for being delivered, and one is still roaming Maharashtra like an unregistered holy cow.
  • Thirty days to transmit five digital software licence keys across the internet. A carrier pigeon with a USB stick strapped to its leg could have flown to Seattle, raised a family, and returned before Microsoft 365 arrived.
  • Six days for an ergonomic plastic mouse to travel across town, while internal tracking swore on its grandmother’s grave that I personally begged them to detain it in a warehouse.
  • One laptop stand that failed the “product delivery guidelines” for existing, was summarily cancelled, repromised for next week, and stamped “resolved” today.
  • One beard trimmer still described as “out for delivery” forty-eight hours past its deadline, presumably shaving extraterrestrial life in another galaxy.
  • One caller-ID warning that the delivery driver might show up as a telephone scammer ending in 777. Ordering from Flipkart is basically Russian roulette, except every chamber contains an automated apology.
  • And an executive escalation to two billionaire chief executives that produced the exact same result as shouting your complaint into an unplugged microwave.

They say patience is a virtue. At Flipkart, it’s a clinical diagnosis. Flipkart BLACK promised 24/7 priority support with “no queue, no bot.” As it turns out, that wasn’t a product feature—it was a confession. There was no queue because nobody was coming, and there was no bot because an actual bot would have had the decency to feel embarrassed.

Flipkart’s dashboard says my complaints are “resolved.” And you know what? They’re completely right. I’ve resolved never to shop with them again.

Quick questions.

For anyone contemplating ordering from Flipkart.

Did the orders ever actually arrive?

Three did. The mouse arrived on 21 October, six days late. The extender arrived on 29 October, seven days late. Flipkart’s notices reported the five Microsoft licences delivered on 25 October, thirty days after the order. The Acer stand was cancelled on 4 November and replaced by an item expected on 11 November. The Philips trimmer was ordered on 28 October, expected on 4 November, and was still “out for delivery” on 6 November. Two of five have not been delivered in any sense a courier would recognise.

What does “resolved” mean at Flipkart?

On 6 November, the delivery complaint about an undelivered laptop stand was marked “resolved”, even though the stand had not arrived and its replacement was not expected until 11 November. The word appears to describe the administrative comfort of a case file, not the physical state of a parcel. Meanwhile, the Logitech issue was still marked “open” more than two weeks after the mouse had already been delivered. It is the only vocabulary in which a delivered product remains unresolved and a missing one is resolved.

Did anyone at Flipkart or Walmart ever reply?

Not in the captured mail. The grievance of 17 October went to the grievance mailbox. The escalations of 20 and 28 October went to the Group CEO of Flipkart, the President and CEO of Walmart, the grievance officer, and the Walmart directors’ address. Through 6 November the replies were automated incident updates, plus one phone call that could discuss only the mouse that had already been delivered. No named recipient wrote back in the captured mail, and the grievance was twenty days old with no acknowledgement there.

Why didn’t you simply cancel?

Because canceling an order requires interacting with the very same interface that insisted I rescheduled my own deliveries, passed me across four separate departments, and claimed I had never contacted them in the first place. Attempting to click “Cancel” would almost certainly have triggered three replacement shipments, four notices of lost couriers, a delivery executive calling from a number ending in 777 asking for a landmark near a peepal tree, and an automated email appointing me Vice President of Logistics.

Is this an official website?

No. This is an independent customer account documenting one individual’s actual correspondence between September and November 2026. If this were an official Flipkart website, this page would have been promised for yesterday, marked as rescheduled at your request, and marked resolved while still out for delivery on a bullock cart.
