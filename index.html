From delayed Flipkart deliveries to unanswered questions

I placed five orders and bought a membership. What followed was a masterclass in bureaucratic theatre, algorithmic gaslighting, and courier metaphysics.

Four distinct acts:

The transaction

I paid real rupees. Flipkart gave me dates intended purely as desktop feng shui.

The horoscope

Dates vanished. Tracking claimed I personally requested to delay my own orders.

The VIP darshan

Paid "no bot" priority support immediately queued me behind 64 people to chat with a bot.

The resolution

Two complaints marked "resolved" on 6 November. One of them concerned a stand still expected on 11 November.

On Flipkart BLACK priority support

Promised 24x7 priority support with "no queue, no bot." In reality: a VIP darshan barricade where an algorithm named after a deity tells you to try again next year. Six weeks of escalations later, nobody has explained when the benefit actually starts.

One customer.
Many tomorrows.

An independent account · September–November 2026

12 min read

In standard economic theory, e-commerce is a simple transaction: you enter your CVV, complete the OTP dance, and eventually a courier on a Hero Splendor calls asking for a landmark near a dairy booth to hand you a cardboard box.

Flipkart does not believe in standard economic theory. Flipkart believes that purchasing a computer mouse should be an epic six-week spiritual pilgrimage into the relativity of space, time, and Indian postal philosophy.

Across those six weeks, five orders produced a steady stream of status updates, three near-identical notices about a parcel that might be lost or damaged, an impromptu fruit spelling examination of a corporate bot, two complaints marked “resolved” on the same day, and an escalation to the leadership of Flipkart and Walmart that moved with all the thundering speed of a continental shelf. The emails arrived with relentless speed; only the packages struggled with the basic laws of locomotion.

The airport scene and product illustrations on this page are visual aids, not actual photographs of parcels wandering through Maharashtra.

The scenic route.

Five orders as recorded in Flipkart’s dispatch notices and account pages.

The mouse

Logitech MX Vertical

Promised for 15 October, delivered on 21 October.

Six days late for an in-stock plastic ergonomic mouse. But the real artistic masterpiece was the internal tracking update: “Delivery rescheduled as per customer request.”

Think about the sheer audacity of this fiction. Who is the customer who wakes up on a Tuesday morning, stares at the ceiling fan, and thinks: “You know what? My wrist is feeling far too comfortable. Let me call Flipkart and request that my ergonomic mouse be detained at a warehouse for six more days”? Did I sleepwalk? Did I send an astral projection to the delivery hub to tell the courier uncle to take a holiday? If I had the psychic ability to reschedule shipments into the future, I would have used it to make the mouse arrive last week.

The licences

Microsoft 365 Personal × 5

Ordered 25 September, delivery reported on 25 October.

Thirty full days. Seven hundred and twenty hours. To transmit an electronic software activation key across the internet.

In thirty days, an Indian student can join a coaching institute, solve ten years of physics question papers, and have an existential breakdown. In thirty days, a caterpillar becomes a butterfly. In thirty days, you could walk on foot from Bengaluru to Delhi, hand-carve the Microsoft Excel product keys onto a marble slab, and carry it back. What was the digital key doing for a month? Was it stuck in traffic at Silk Board junction? Did the 25-character license code stop at an Udipi hotel for masala dosa?

The order page eventually listed five delivered entries and four cancelled entries for those five licences. Nine outcomes. The replacement request was marked complete, which settles the paperwork without entirely settling the question of where anything is.

The extender

TP-Link Wi-Fi range extender

Due 22 October, delivered on 29 October.

Due on 22 October. At 1:19 a.m. on 23 October—seventy-nine minutes after the day had officially died—my phone buzzed with an email cheerfully informing me that my delivery for yesterday was delayed.

This is not advance tracking. This is an unprompted 2 a.m. drunk text from Flipkart saying, “Hey... so about yesterday... it didn't work out.” Telling a customer that yesterday didn't happen is not logistics; it is historical fiction. That is not advance notice. That is the delivery calendar emailing its condolences from the afterlife.

The stand

Acer ZC.A01SI.179 laptop stand

Promised for 21 October, moved to 22 October, then repromised at intervals until the word stopped meaning anything. The support chat called it “systemic repromise being done.” I have kept the phrase. It is the only honest sentence in the archive.

On 4 November, thirty-two minutes apart, Flipkart sent two explanations. The first said the parcel “may have been lost or damaged while on its way to you” and that the seller would ship a new item. The second said it “could not be successfully delivered as it did not meet the product delivery guidelines” and that the seller would work on a replacement. A laptop stand had failed to meet the guidelines for being a laptop stand.

The original order was cancelled. A replacement appeared, expected on 11 November. On 6 November the delivery complaint was marked “resolved.” The complaint and the stand now run on separate schedules, and only one of them has arrived.

The trimmer

Philips MG3760/33 multigroomer

Ordered 28 October, expected 4 November. On 6 November the order page still described it as “out for delivery.”

A parcel still described as “out for delivery” two days after its expected date is not out for delivery. It has moved out, forwarded its mail, and is pursuing other interests. The chat, meanwhile, raised a priority ticket and promised a definitive update on WhatsApp by 6 p.m. I asked the reasonable follow-up question: “Till end of time?” The answer was that the update would arrive by 6 p.m.

The bot then typed “Please ignore above line”, the first recorded instance of a corporation withdrawing a sentence in real time. The captured records contain no delivery confirmation.

The email trail.

Excerpts from private correspondence. Commentary is mine; absurdity was Flipkart’s.

14 October 2026

Three emails, one quantum parcel

Flipkart wrote to inform me that my Microsoft shipment “may have been lost or damaged while on its way to you” and a replacement would be dispatched. By 17 October, I had received three near-identical notices, while no replacement had actually left the depot.

“Lost or damaged” is a magnificent phrase. It spans the entire philosophical spectrum of non-existence. The package might have fallen into an open Bangalore storm drain; it might have been consumed by a sacred cow; it might have dissolved into pure radiant energy. What it was categorically not doing was arriving in my inbox. Three near-identical notices that your parcel is theoretically destroyed is wonderful if you collect automated corporate apologies, but unhelpful when you actually need to open a spreadsheet.

17 October 2026

The customer who never was

Internal tracking marked my orders rescheduled “at customer request.” I had spent a week sending frantic messages pleading with anyone with a pulse to put the box on a two-wheeler.

To then receive an automated update claiming that I personally petitioned the company to delay my own order is gaslighting of the highest order. It is like being slapped across the face by a stranger who then turns to the crowd and announces: “He asked me to do that, he's very fond of cheek stimulation.” If my pending orders have developed the legal autonomy to negotiate their own delivery dates, Flipkart should immediately put them on the board of directors.

That day I sent a grievance headed “3 orders undelivered, wrong dates blamed on me - please fix” to the grievance mailbox. I asked for the products, the correction of the records, and one human whose phone works. It was a modest list. No acknowledgement appears in the captured mail.

20 October 2026

Escalating to the stratosphere

Having exhausted the chatbots, I escalated directly to Kalyan Krishnamurthy, Group CEO of Flipkart, and John Furner, President and CEO of Walmart Inc. I copied the Flipkart grievance mailbox and the Walmart directors' address, attached order numbers and dates, and asked for a single accountable human.

An email thread with that distribution list could ratify an international extradition treaty. The job titles had reached the stratosphere. The reply, that same day, came from Customer Support, concerned the Logitech mouse, and requested time until 21 October, 4:23 p.m. It did not mention the email, the grievance, or either CEO, which is one way to keep a conversation focused.

20 October 2026

The strawberry Turing test

Having purchased a 12-month Flipkart BLACK membership promising “Priority Customer Support 24x7 no queue no bot,” I opened the priority channel. I was immediately deposited into a waiting line behind dozens of other priority members.

To establish whether I was conversing with a sentient human being or an uninspired algorithm running on a potato, I asked a simple diagnostic question: “How many times does the letter 'r' appear in the word 'strawberry'?”

The priority agent confidently typed: “Two.”

There are three. While an exhausted human can certainly miscount, a human does not follow up a miscount by instantly copy-pasting three consecutive paragraphs of boilerplate text instructing me to clear my Chrome cache, restart my router, and re-download the Flipkart app. If this is “no queue, no bot,” I would be terrified to see what Flipkart considers an actual bot. It probably demands Aadhaar verification before typing a full stop.

23 October 2026

The specialist who solved yesterday

I finally reached a living, breathing human representative on the phone. They were exceedingly polite, confirmed they could see my Logitech mouse order, and then gently explained that my other pending orders belonged to other teams and could not be discussed on that call.

The mouse had already been delivered forty-eight hours earlier. I had successfully pierced the corporate veil only to speak with an agent whose jurisdiction was strictly confined to the only problem that no longer existed. If Flipkart operated an emergency ambulance service, the paramedics would arrive exclusively at the homes of people who had already made a full recovery and were currently eating parathas.

After the call I wrote to both CEOs again, because the agent had told me to expect contact from other teams. No written reply followed. The mouse, at least, had been delivered. Its department had done its job, and its jurisdiction was now closed.

24 October 2026

The address I did not change

Two emails arrived in the small hours, at 2:06 and 2:08 a.m., headed “Address updated” — one for the TP-Link order, one for the Acer stand.

I had not updated an address. I had spent the week asking for the audit trail of the earlier date changes recorded in my name, which were still on my account. Flipkart had responded by creating a second record I could dispute. The address was the same address. It is now officially a different version of itself.

The audit trail has not arrived. In its place, the account eventually marked the TP-Link address-change issue “resolved” on 6 November. Nothing had been requested, so nothing had been undone, and the matter is now finished. It is the cleanest piece of administration in the entire file.

26 October 2026

The lunch-break denial

“We see you havent contacted our support team about your concern.”

An automated email arrived declaring my case was now “top priority” and a specialist would call within 24 hours. Naturally, that call never materialized.

Instead, a formal declaration arrived from the Grievance Officer: “We see you havent contacted our support team about your concern.” This was printed directly at the bottom of an email thread containing fourteen prior support messages from Flipkart agents.

This is the absolute pinnacle of Indian bureaucratic theatre. It is the spiritual equivalent of walking up to an inquiry counter, presenting your passbook, and being told: “Aapka account hi nahi hai, lunch ke baad aana.” Entering the grievance department is like stepping through a wormhole: all preceding history is wiped clean. The previous fourteen customer service representatives who wrote to me had apparently done so purely in a personal capacity as pen pals. To prove I had contacted support, I would need to forward the support thread to the grievance desk, who would almost certainly reply to inform me that email had not yet been invented.

28–29 October 2026

The notice, and the delay it suffered

On 28 October I wrote to both CEOs and the grievance mailbox under the subject “Two paid orders undelivered, grievance of 17 October unanswered, three escalations with no reply, legal notice.” The subject line was doing a lot of work, but it was accurate.

The email asked for a named owner, dispatch dates, the order-event audit trail, answers on the BLACK membership, and a reply by 5 p.m. on 30 October. It cited the 48-hour acknowledgement I understood the e-commerce rules to require. It also said I intended to pursue a consumer complaint. That is not a legal finding; it is a person writing a very long email and pressing send.

The next day, Flipkart reported delivering the Wi-Fi extender. The extender had been due on 22 October. Whatever moved it — the notice, the planetary alignment, or a courier finally locating the correct street — the parcel shifted the day after the words “Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission” entered the thread.

When I tried to send a follow-up to the grievance address, Gmail bounced back: “Temporary System Problem... Delivery delayed... will continue trying for 46 hours.” The cosmic loop was complete. My complaint about delivery delays had suffered a delivery delay. Somewhere, a mail server had adopted the local management style.

2 November 2026

The reply was mine

“I give up, it seems no one at Flipkart or Walmart gives a damn about FK Customers. Thanks for the cautionary tale. Bess your heart.”

Sixteen days after the grievance, thirteen after the CEO escalation, and five after the notice, the thread contained no message from a named recipient. I closed it myself.

The typo is mine. I stand by it. A company that will not answer deserves correspondence of equivalent care.

1–4 November 2026

Three deadlines, one concerned team

Three incident emails arrived. All three opened identically: “We have escalated your issue to the concerned team.” I have begun to picture the concerned team as a small, extremely busy office with no phone, no headcount, and no output, staffed entirely by the word “concerned.”

Each promised to resolve the problem by 7 November: at 11:55 a.m., at 6:37 p.m., and at 9:00 p.m. Three deadlines for one day is not a schedule. It is a raffle with apologies.

The Logitech support issue remained open throughout. Its card read: “Open | Resolution by Tomorrow, Nov 07”. The app has invented a new tense in which tomorrow is a fixed calendar date. The mouse had been delivered two weeks earlier and was, by then, the only participant in this story with nothing left to resolve.

4 November 2026

Two diagnoses, thirty-two minutes

At 9:41 p.m. on 4 November, Flipkart wrote that the Acer stand “may have been lost or damaged while on its way to you” and that a new item would be shipped. At 10:13 p.m., it wrote that the item “could not be successfully delivered as it did not meet the product delivery guidelines”, and that the seller would work on a replacement.

Two diagnoses, one missing parcel. “Lost or damaged” is a flexible condition; “did not meet the product delivery guidelines” is a bureaucratic way of saying the parcel was rejected by the concept of delivery. A laptop stand had failed the entrance exam for arriving.

The original order was cancelled and a replacement was scheduled for 11 November. On 6 November the delivery complaint was marked resolved. The complaint took less time to resolve than the stand has taken to exist.

6 November 2026

The word “resolved”

On 6 November the account’s issue list showed two cards as “resolved”: the Acer delivery complaint and the TP-Link address change. The Acer replacement was still expected on 11 November. The TP-Link order had been delivered on 29 October, the day before the 30 October date the chat had given.

The address change had never been requested. The stand had never arrived. Both were now resolved, which suggests that in this vocabulary the word describes the state of the file rather than the state of the world.

At the same review, the Logitech support issue was still open, with a deadline of 7 November. The mouse had arrived on 21 October. One case about a delivered mouse was open; one case about an undelivered stand was closed; one case about a change nobody made was closed. The grievance of 17 October had no acknowledgement in the captured mail.

Somewhere in this filing system there is a definition of “resolved” that would keep a philosophy department occupied for a decade. I have not been shown it. I have been shown the label.

6 November 2026

The leadership file

Written to: the grievance mailbox, 17 October. The Group CEO of Flipkart and the President and CEO of Walmart, 20 October. Both of them again, plus the grievance officer and the Walmart directors’ address, 28 October. A closing reply to the same thread, 2 November.

Received in reply, as of 6 November: automated incident updates from Customer Support, one phone call about a mouse that had already arrived, and no message from any named recipient. No acknowledgement or closure notice for the grievance appears in the captured mail through 6 November.

There is a difference between an email being received and a company being reached. Flipkart has mastered the first.

Quick questions.

For anyone contemplating ordering from Flipkart.

Did the orders ever actually arrive?

Three did. The mouse arrived on 21 October, six days late. The extender arrived on 29 October, seven days late. Flipkart’s notices reported the five Microsoft licences delivered on 25 October, thirty days after the order. The Acer stand was cancelled on 4 November and replaced by an item expected on 11 November. The Philips trimmer was ordered on 28 October, expected on 4 November, and was still “out for delivery” on 6 November. Two of five have not been delivered in any sense a courier would recognise.

What does “resolved” mean at Flipkart?

On 6 November, two issue cards were marked “resolved”: an address change nobody requested, and a delivery complaint about a stand that had not arrived and was not expected until 11 November. The word appears to describe the administrative state of a case file, not the physical state of a parcel. Meanwhile the Logitech issue was still “open” more than two weeks after the mouse arrived. It is the only vocabulary in which a delivered product remains unresolved and a missing one is resolved.

Did anyone at Flipkart or Walmart ever reply?

Not in the captured mail. The grievance of 17 October went to the grievance mailbox. The escalations of 20 and 28 October went to the Group CEO of Flipkart, the President and CEO of Walmart, the grievance officer, and the Walmart directors’ address. Through 6 November the replies were automated incident updates, plus one phone call that could discuss only the mouse that had already been delivered. No named recipient wrote back in the captured mail, and the grievance was twenty days old with no acknowledgement there.

Why didn’t you simply cancel?

Because canceling an order requires interacting with the very same interface that insisted I rescheduled my own deliveries, passed me across four separate departments, and claimed I had never contacted them in the first place. Attempting to click "Cancel" would almost certainly have triggered three replacement shipments, four notices of lost couriers, a delivery boy calling at 6:30 a.m. asking for a landmark near a peepal tree, and an automated email appointing me Vice President of Logistics.

Is this an official website?

No. This is an independent customer account documenting one individual’s actual correspondence between September and November 2026. If this were an official Flipkart website, this page would have been promised for yesterday, marked as rescheduled at your request, and marked resolved while still out for delivery on a bullock cart.
